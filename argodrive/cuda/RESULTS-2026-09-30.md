# CUDA SSD-streaming results, 2026-09-30

Box: one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell 96 GB (passthrough), Threadripper PRO 9955WX (16 vCPU), 60 GiB VM RAM,
3x WD SN8100 RAID0 (2 MiB chunk) exposed to the VM as one virtio-scsi LV, Samsung 9100 PRO system disk.
Model: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash Q4 (519 GB GGUF, 40 layers, 384 routed experts per layer, 19 MiB per expert).
Serving: `ds4-server --cuda --ssd-streaming --ssd-streaming-cache-experts 4600 --ctx 8192`, one GPU.
Harness: OpenAI-compatible requests; 512-token prompt / 400 generated tokens; prompts fixed per run
(seeds 11-15 or 21-24) so arms compare the same prompts; every arm also runs a temperature-0 request
whose SHA-256 must equal the unmodified build's (81e7ab1fbdfc). Decode = generated tokens / (server time -
prefill time). Same-config spread is about +/-1 tok/s, so anything under 5% needs an A/B/B/A.

## Measured decode partition (2026-10-01 05:07 UTC, 85,399 decode layers, 800-token generations, production stack)
Per layer: router-id readback (= GPU drain) 0.170 ms; host expert phase 0.822 ms, of which reads + issue 0.612 ms and
DMA wait 0.206 ms; 0.80 misses per layer. Per 40-layer token: readback 6.8 ms, host phase 32.9 ms, against a measured
token of ~45 ms (22 tok/s). The GPU kernels are therefore a minor term; the expert read path (bandwidth-bound at
~33 GB/s per 19 MiB expert over virtio) and the exposed DMA tail are the critical path. Pool-capacity curve from the
real-traffic histogram: 42.1% of misses captured at 2,156 slots (today), 60.4% at 4,096; with a miss at ~0.6 ms vs a pool
hit at ~0.37 ms, a 76 GB pool is worth ~+3%, not the +23% it gave when a miss cost 1.9 ms.

## 2026-10-05: prefill profiled and fixed (+66% on long prompts), decode read path measured to its floor

Harness as before (OpenAI-compatible requests, fixed prompt seeds, temperature-0 hash per arm), now at the
production context of 32768 (3,731 VRAM expert slots). "4.9k-token prompts" are the invoice-list prompts of
the earlier "2.5k" arms, which tokenise to 4,916 tokens and prefill in three 2,048-token chunks.

### What the prefill profile showed

An nsys capture of a cold 2,349-token prefill (11.1 s, 212 tok/s): GPU kernels ran 7.2 s, of which the two
Q4_K routed-MoE kernels `moe_gate_up_mid_q4K_expert_tile8_rowspan_kernel` and
`moe_down_q4K_expert_tile8_rowspan_kernel` took 5.1 s (72% of GPU time, about 8 TFLOPS effective). The
disk read 90.7 GB during the prefill at an average of only 7.3 GB/s; staging and DMA waits were negligible.
Attention was 1% of GPU time. Per layer the host blocks on the router-id readback, loads the missing experts
with the GPU idle, then launches the MoE; nothing overlaps.

Root cause of the slow kernels: they stage the quantised activations of a tile in shared memory only for
inputs of at most 16 (gate/up) or 8 (down) Q8_K blocks, and the INT8 tensor-core variants are admitted
only under the same limits. Those are V4 Flash's dimensions (n_embd 4096, n_ff_exp 2048). V4.1 Flash has
n_embd 5120 and n_ff_exp 2304, so on this model every lane re-read the activations from global memory for
each row and the tensor-core kernels never ran.

### Qualified (A/B/B/A, five fixed prompts per arm, bit-exact in every arm)

| Change | Flag | 4,916-token prefill | 512-token prefill | Decode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 20/9 activation blocks in the scalar expert-tile kernels | (always on) | 200 / 200 -> 277 / 273 (**+37%**) | 143 / 143 -> 174 / 180 (**+24%**) | flat |
| Exact INT8 tensor-core expert tiles admitted for 20/9 blocks | `DS4_CUDA_MOE_Q4_MMA_WIDE=1` | 277 / 275 -> 332 / 332 (**+20%**) | 182 / 178 -> 196 / 202 (**+10%**) | flat |

Cumulative against the previous production build: prefill 200 -> 332 tok/s on 4.9k-token prompts (+66%) and
143 -> ~200 on 512-token prompts (+39%). Both changes keep the per-output arithmetic and reduction order of the
reference kernels; the 120-token answer hash (81e7ab1fbdfc) and a 2.3k-token-prompt answer hash (338f5ea8108c)
were identical in all arms. The tensor-core kernels' own comment states they were fuzz-verified bit-identical to
the scalar tiles; the limits were sizing, not numerics.

### Decode: the host phase, measured

Per-miss-type timers in the load loop (97k decode layers, 1.07 VRAM misses per layer): pool hits cost 6 us
each to issue; NVMe misses 0.56 per layer at **1.03 ms each** (read+issue), which is the whole "reads+issue"
interval; victim scans 4 us, lookups 1 us, everything else under 5 us per layer. An nsys capture of 120
steady-state decode tokens (KV cached, 19.5 tok/s): kernels 16.9 ms per token (GPU busy 32%); the decode
thread spends 10.2 ms waiting for expert uploads, 6.2 ms issuing 2,864 kernel launches (72 per layer, about
20 of them sub-microsecond bf16 roundings), 5.6 ms in blocking copies (the readback waits), 4.1 ms in the
per-token device synchronisations. The large dense GEMVs already run at 1.2-1.5 TB/s.

Read-path sweep with the per-read timer (ms per 19 MiB expert): primary weight 4 1.077, 3 1.046, 2 1.021,
weight 4 with 2 sub-reads 1.036, weight 3 with 2 sub-reads 1.028: flat within 5%. The virtio array path has a
floor near 1 ms per expert whatever the request shape; it is not queue-depth-limited. Weight 4 stays.

### Two-pass prefill MoE (`DS4_CUDA_PREFILL_WAVES=1`): qualified, +5%

The routed MoE for pairs whose experts are already resident is launched before the host loads the misses,
so the GPU computes while the disk and DMA work; a second pass runs the loaded experts; the fixed-order sum
runs once after both. Per-pair writes are disjoint and the sum order unchanged, so the result is bit-exact by
construction (both hashes identical in every arm). Three implementation faults were found and fixed on the
way: the single pass keeps the quantised activations in the down-output buffer, which pass 1 overwrites; the
first fix allocated a separate buffer in a VRAM that is 99% full; and the launcher rewrites the expert table
for the cache after the first load, so pass 2 must reuse the table the first load was given.

| Arm | 4,916-token prefill | 512-token prefill | Decode |
|---|---|---|---|
| single pass (tensor-core tiles on) | 331 / 333 | 202 / 195 | 17.5 / 17.5 |
| two-pass (73-75% of resident pairs ran under the loads) | 348 / 352 (**+5.5%**) | 208 / 208 (**+5%**) | 17.2 / 17.7 |

The gain is bounded by the GPU phase, which the kernel fixes already made small. What remains of a
4.9k-token prefill is the loader reading missing experts one at a time at ~1 ms each (about 120 per
2,048-token layer-chunk), roughly 6 GB/s against a 30 GB/s path; batched reads are the next lever.

### 2026-10-06 later: audit instrumentation, and an expert-contiguous layout that does not help

Three diagnostics were added (always on, no measurable cost):

- **Per-source expert read tails.** For every component read the array piece and the replica pieces are timed
  separately and the last finisher recorded. Over 180,000 reads during decode: the array piece of a split
  gate/up component averages 0.845 ms and finishes last in **97.9%** of reads; the array-only down component
  averages 0.926 ms; the replica piece is faster but spikes to 9.6 ms occasionally.
- **Prefill sweep summary** per layer-chunk: tokens, unique experts, misses split into pool hits and NVMe reads,
  and load+DMA time. A cached follow-up turn that forwards 326 tokens needs ~135 unique experts per layer and
  71-76 misses (48-56 from NVMe), which is 2.0-2.3 s of its 2.7-3.0 s prompt time. The single-token tail path
  (threshold 18 tokens, now logged at startup) never applies to such turns.
- **Expert pack reads** counter for the option below.

`DS4_CUDA_EXPERT_PACK=<file>` reads a missing expert as **one** O_DIRECT request from an expert-contiguous copy
of the routed experts (gate|up|down of each expert adjacent, with a per-layer index file), instead of three
concurrent component reads. The bytes land in the same slots, so the output is unchanged.

An offline replay of real expert reads had screened this as a 25% win (0.96 ms versus 1.28 ms per expert, with a
much tighter tail). In the engine it is flat:

| Arm | 4,916-token prefill | 512-token prefill | Decode | Hashes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| component reads | 349 / 348 | 204 / 204 | 19.94 / 20.38 | identical |
| pack reads (107,000 per arm) | 350 / 352 | 204 / 206 | 19.66 / 19.11 | identical |

The replay was confounded by write age: its sample pack had been written seconds earlier. A controlled
comparison over the same experts settles it:

| Read | Mean | p50 | p95 |
|---|---|---|---|
| three components concurrent from the model file (settled) | 1.549 ms | 1.510 | 1.857 |
| one 19 MiB request from the full 306 GB pack (settled) | 1.639 ms | 1.632 | 1.886 |
| one 19 MiB request from a 4 GB file written moments before | 1.370 ms | 1.355 | 1.441 |

On settled data a single large request is slightly slower than three concurrent ones. Physical adjacency of an
expert's components buys nothing here; the earlier screening gain was the drives' write cache. Anyone measuring
this storage path should compare only files of equal write age.

Also measured off: a full replica (adding the down tensors to the second device) with the weight shifted toward
it, judged by the per-expert timer: 1.047 ms with gate/up at weight 4, then 1.053 (weight 4), 1.076 (weight 3),
1.217 (weight 2) with the full replica. Its 1.3 MiB piece already costs ~0.8 ms, so both devices are
latency-bound at these sizes and moving bytes between them cannot help. The exact bf16 epilogue fold was
requalified over eight alternating arms: medians 18.33, 18.96, 19.34, 21.48 against 18.86, 18.99, 19.01, 19.12.
No repeatable gain demonstrated; it stays default off.

### 2026-10-06, third pass: the cache policy, and what a speculative verification step would cost

Three questions, all answered with measurements; nothing promoted.

**1. Is the pinned pool's ranking any good?** The production ranking was collected while a ranked pool was
already loaded and `DS4_CUDA_POOL_EVICT_BIAS` was active. That bias evicts pool-backed VRAM slots first, so
pool members miss VRAM again and their miss counts rise: the ranking reinforces itself, and an earlier
re-collection reproduced it almost exactly. An unbiased histogram (no preload, bias 0, 400,000 VRAM misses
over 14,606 experts on real traffic) shows why that does not matter:

| Slots | Share of VRAM misses captured by the top N |
|---|---|
| 862 | 11.7% |
| 2,156 (the pool's size) | 26.9% |
| 4,000 | 46.0% |
| 8,000 | 78.4% |

The distribution is nearly flat (the most-missed expert accounts for 74 of 400,000 misses), so the best
possible static ranking of 2,156 slots would capture 26.9%. Production measures **39-40%**, above that
ceiling, because the pool and the eviction bias work as a pair rather than as a static prior. An A/B of the
two rankings confirms it: capture 39.4 / 39.8% with the production list against 39.9 / 40.4% with the
unbiased list, decode medians 20.70 / 19.10 against 19.07 / 19.60. Any list converges to the same
equilibrium, so the preload only saves the cold-start fill. Pool-ranking work is finished; a future attempt
needs a new policy, not a new list.

**2. Should prefill-loaded experts be kept in VRAM for the next turn?** (`DS4_CUDA_PREFILL_COLD_AGE=N`, added
here, default 2 = today's behaviour.) Raising the demotion stamp to 200 does what it should: within one
conversation the second and third follow-ups prefill 26-27% faster and their NVMe misses nearly halve
(55.7 -> 29.8 per layer). But it costs 1.8 s on the first prompt and 1.7 s on the first follow-up, so a
five-turn conversation nets out slightly worse. On the production pattern of unrelated questions it is worse
still: the six prompt times of three independent two-turn conversations sum to 48.66 / 48.90 s against
45.93 / 46.30 s at the default. Stamp 2000 is worse everywhere (VRAM fills with stale prefill experts:
219 misses per layer on a later turn). Default off.

**3. What would a speculative verification step cost?** A batched forward over a cached prefix is exactly a
verification step, so its cost can be measured without building anything. Extending the same 2,048-token
checkpoint by k tokens (`max_tokens=1`):

| k | Forward | Per token | Decode steps of equal time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.352 s | 117 ms | 7 |
| 6 | 0.446 s | 74 ms | 9 |
| 12 | 0.660 s | 55 ms | 13 |
| 18 | 0.633 s | 35 ms | 12 |
| 34 | 0.794 s | 23 ms | 16 |
| 51 | 1.185 s | 23 ms | 23 |
| 256 | 1.241 s | 4.9 ms | 24 |
| 402 | 1.805 s | 4.5 ms | 35 |

At a 51 ms decode token, verification breaks even near **k = 13** and only pays above it: 34 tokens for the
price of 16, 256 for the price of 24. The fixed cost is the expert loading a batch forces, since the union of
experts a batch needs grows quickly with k and saturates near all 384 per layer. Consequently the usual
speculative widths of 4 to 8 would be *slower* here than plain decoding even with perfect acceptance, and a
width of 32 at 50% acceptance only breaks even. Speculation on this deployment would need wide drafts
accepted nearly in full; it is not a promising direction while experts stream from storage.
